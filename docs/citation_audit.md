## Method and limitations

Every footnote in the submitted PDF was extracted verbatim and each bibliographic item checked against publisher catalogues, library and indexer records, journal tables of contents, and review notices. Where possible each item was corroborated from **two or more independent sources** (e.g. a publisher listing *and* a review in a peer-reviewed journal; a Philosophy Documentation Center record *and* the author's own publication list).

**Two limitations must be stated plainly, because they bound the confidence of some rows below:**

1. **Direct API and publisher-site access was unavailable from this environment.** The Crossref REST API, WorldCat, Cambridge Core, Brill, Bloomsbury, Project MUSE, JSTOR and the SAET site were all blocked at the network layer. Verification therefore proceeded through search-surfaced records of those same sources rather than through direct queries to them. Where a record could only be traced to a single source, the row is marked accordingly.
2. **Three classes of item resist online verification altogether** and are marked *Unverifiable* rather than *Not found*: internal page references to books (footnotes 9–14, 27), the pagination of chapters in volumes without an open table of contents, and articles in *Hill Road*, a Chinese-language Hong Kong seminary journal that is not indexed in Crossref and whose back-issue contents are not fully published online.

**Nothing in footnotes 1–36 appears to be fabricated.** Every work cited exists. The problems identified below are problems of accuracy, attribution, and quotation integrity — a materially different finding from invention, and one the editor should weigh differently.

## Direct quotations and attributed terms

These three checks matter more than most of the table above, because each one puts words inside quotation marks that the manuscript attributes to a named author.

### 1. Badiou, *Saint Paul*, p. 3 — the "subjective through and through" quotation

**As quoted (fn 12, p. 5 of the MS):** "My intention, clearly… is subjective through and through."

**Source text:** "My intention, clearly, is neither historicizing nor exegetical. **It** is subjective through and through."

**Status: Corrected.** The words are genuine, but the ellipsis silently collapses two sentences and deletes the clause "is neither historicizing nor exegetical." That deletion is not neutral. The manuscript deploys the quotation to show that Badiou "explicitly brack[ets] its theological content" — but the suppressed clause is a disclaimer about *historical and exegetical method*, not about theology. The sentence as Badiou wrote it supports a weaker and different claim than the one it is made to carry here. Recommend quoting both sentences in full.

**Page reference:** the passage sits in the Prologue immediately after Badiou's remark about "a Lenin for whom Christ will have been the equivocal Marx," which is customarily cited at **p. 2**. The manuscript's "p. 3" may well be right in the Stanford pagination, but it should be re-checked against the printed edition.

### 2. "Nomadic proletarians"

**As quoted (fn 5 / MS p. 3):** the migrant as "an emblem of what he calls the 'nomadic proletarians'."

**Status: Corrected.** Badiou's term in the Litvak translation is the collective **"nomadic proletariat"**, used in the singular of an individual as **"a nomadic proletarian"** (Polity's own description: "the 'migrant', argues Badiou, is in fact a nomadic proletarian"). The plural noun form "nomadic proletarians" matches neither. Since the phrase appears inside quotation marks and is glossed as "what he calls," it should be brought into line with the published translation and given a page.

### 3. "Founder of universalism"

**As written (MS p. 4):** "his earlier engagement with Saint Paul, whom he famously describes as the 'founder of universalism'."

**Status: Not found as a quotation.** No evidence was located that Badiou anywhere calls Paul the "founder of universalism." The book's subtitle is *The Foundation of Universalism* (Fr. *La fondation de l'universalisme*) — a nominalisation about the *founding*, which is not the same claim as naming Paul its *founder*. Badiou's own characterisations of Paul in the text run to "poet-thinker of the event," "antiphilosophical theoretician of universality," and militant subject. The manuscript's phrase reads as a paraphrase of the subtitle presented as a quotation. **Recommend either removing the quotation marks or supplying a page reference.**

### 4. The claim attributed to Thomas Nail — *the most serious item in this audit*

**As written (fn 8):** "Thomas Nail goes so far as to claim that Badiou 'offers nothing new,' instead restating an anti-capitalist position."

**Status: Not found / Unverifiable.** The article is real and correctly cited (see fn 8b above). But nothing I could locate supports this characterisation of its argument, and the available evidence points the other way:

- The review's own framing describes it as "an introduction to Badiou's vision of migrant justice as part of new communist politics" — expository, not dismissive.
- Nail's published position on Badiou elsewhere is markedly positive: in his work on Badiou and the *sans-papiers* he calls Badiou's contribution, with L'Organisation politique, "absolutely crucial," lying "at the practical and theoretical roots of much of non-status migrant organizing today." His criticism is that this work "needs to be revised and updated" in light of more recent struggles — a call for extension, not a charge of redundancy.
- The full text is paywalled at the Philosophy Documentation Center and was not reachable from this environment, so I cannot rule out that the phrase appears somewhere in the five pages.

Because the phrase is set in quotation marks and attributed by name, the author should be asked to supply **the exact sentence and page**. If it cannot be produced, the sentence should be cut or rewritten, since as it stands it places an evaluative judgement in a living scholar's mouth on the strength of a five-page review that appears to say something close to the opposite.

### 5. So Jung Kim, *A Vernacular Theology of Diaspora* — publication status

The book is dated 2026 and appears in retailer listings from around April 2026. Given the manuscript's own submission date, the author should confirm whether the volume was actually published — as opposed to announced — at the time of writing, and whether they consulted it or a pre-publication description. This matters because the citation supports a substantive claim about how diaspora theology "frame[s] ethnic heritage as a theological resource," and because the publisher attribution in the note is wrong (Lexington/Fortress Academic, not Bloomsbury), which is the signature of a reference captured from a listing rather than from the book.

## Summary for the editor

**Headline:** 36 footnotes, 33 distinct bibliographic items. Nothing fabricated. **Nine items require correction, three cannot be verified, and four quotation-level problems need author response** — one of which (the Nail attribution) I would treat as a condition of acceptance rather than a copy-editing matter.

### Pattern 1 — Errors cluster in exactly the Asian-studies references the argument depends on

This is the finding I would put in front of the author first. The manuscript's contribution is its application of Badiou to *diasporic Asian* Christian identity, and the constructive section rests on footnotes 19, 33, 34 and 35. Those four notes contain:

- a wrong given name (**"Eastern Law"** for Easten Law, fn 34);
- a non-standard and probably wrong name form (**"Chi W. Huen"**, fn 35);
- a wrong publisher *and* wrong place (**"Dublin: Bloomsbury"** for Lexington/Fortress Academic, fn 19);
- a wrong year (**2019** for 2018, fn 19);
- an inverted East Asian name (**"Pui-lan Kwok"** for Kwok Pui-lan, fn 18).

By contrast the Pauline-scholarship notes (21, 22, 24, 25) are almost clean, and the migration-theology notes (15, 16) are clean. The distribution suggests these particular items were assembled from secondary lists or catalogue snippets rather than from the works themselves — which is a substantive concern, not a stylistic one, precisely because this is where the essay claims to be original. It is worth asking the author directly which of these five they consulted firsthand.

### Pattern 2 — The Badiou material shows signs of not having been checked against the printed translation

Three independent signals point the same way: the book's title is misspelled **"Miligtants" in all four short-form citations** (fns 4, 5, 6, 8) — the same error copied forward rather than re-typed; the one direct quotation is truncated in a way that changes what it supports; and both terms placed in quotation marks and attributed to Badiou ("nomadic proletarians," "founder of universalism") fail to match the published English. For an essay whose whole argument is an exposition of two Badiou texts, this cluster deserves an explicit query.

### Pattern 3 — A named scholar's position may be misrepresented

See §4 above. Distinct from the other patterns in kind: everything else is recoverable by correction, whereas this one either has a source or does not.

### Pattern 4 — House-style omissions, easily fixed

Systematic across the notes and not worth individual queries: issue numbers missing from all four journal articles (fns 7b, 8b, 20a, 35c); subtitles omitted at first citation (fns 21b, 24b, 29, 35b); no DOIs anywhere, though at least the *NTS*, *Political Theology* and *Radical Philosophy Review* items carry them; place of publication missing (fn 7a); an unclosed parenthesis (fn 36).

### Recommendation

The citation problems are correctable and none of them undermine the essay's argument on its own. But Patterns 1 and 3 together warrant a **major-revision query to the author rather than a copy-editing pass**: the editor should ask for confirmation that the works in footnotes 8, 19, 33, 34 and 35 were consulted firsthand, and should require the Nail quotation to be substantiated or withdrawn before acceptance.
